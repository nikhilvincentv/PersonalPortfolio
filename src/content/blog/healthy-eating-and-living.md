Most of what I write about here involves code or circuit boards. This one doesn't. I'm an event coordinator with [Healthy Eating and Living](https://healthyeatingnliving.com), a community group that runs fundraisers to get fresh food and healthy habits to families who don't always have easy access to either.

The work is small in scale. Our fundraisers delivered more than 25 pounds of fresh produce to families, and in 2025 I organized a tennis-lesson fundraiser where people paid for lessons and the money went toward food. Nobody is writing a paper about 25 pounds of produce. But I've learned more about running a project from this than from most of my technical work.

## Fresh food has a deadline

Canned goods can sit in a garage for a month. Lettuce can't. When you deal with fresh produce, every step is on a timer: when it gets picked up, where it's stored, who drives it, and when families can actually be there to get it. If one of those steps slips by a day, the food goes bad and the whole effort turns into compost.

It's the same lesson I later learned building [AIsle](/blog/aisle-wedding-hackathon): the hard part is rarely any single task. The hard part is that tasks depend on each other, and when one moves, the others have to move with it.

## People show up for something specific

With the tennis fundraiser, the cause gave people a reason to care, but the lesson itself is what got them to sign up. A parent wants their kid to learn a backhand. A beginner wants an excuse to finally try. A concrete thing you get on a weekend morning is easier to say yes to than a cause in the abstract.

I've thought about that a lot since. Every time I pitch a project now, I try to start with what the person in front of me gets, and then explain why it matters.

## Recognition is nice, logistics are the job

In 2025 the group received a [Washington State Volunteer Service Award](https://servewashington.galaxydigital.com/blog/display/wsvsa-2025-winner-healthy-living-and-eating/?blog_id=28468). That felt great, and I'm proud of it. But when I think about this work, the award isn't what comes to mind. I think about pickup times, volunteer schedules, and making sure the food reached families while it was still fresh.

If you're a student who wants to start something in your community, start small enough that you can do every job yourself at least once. You'll understand the whole thing, and you'll know exactly where help would make the biggest difference.
